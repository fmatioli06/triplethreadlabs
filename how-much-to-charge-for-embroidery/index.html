How Much Should I Charge for Embroidery?

It's the question every embroiderer asks, usually while staring at a quote they don't trust. Here's the honest answer: there is no magic number, but there is a method. Learn the method once and you'll never guess at a price again.

The short answer

Your price has to cover your true cost — every material, every minute of labor, overhead — plus a margin that makes the job worth doing, grossed up for any platform fees. That's the whole game:

The pricing formula
True cost = materials + labor + digitizing share + overhead
Sell price = true cost ÷ (1 − target margin − fee %)
Example: $18 true cost, 30% margin target, 10% Etsy fees → $18 ÷ (1 − 0.30 − 0.10) = $18 ÷ 0.60 = $30. That $30 is the price where you actually keep your 30% after Etsy takes its cut.

If that formula looks like work, that's because it is — the first few times. After that it's arithmetic, and the free calculator does it for you. The rest of this guide is about getting the inputs right, because a formula fed with guesses still gives you a guess.

The 6 costs beginners forget

Almost every underpriced quote traces back to one of these. Beginners count the blank and the thread and stop. Working shops count all six:

  1. Thread. Roughly $0.25–$0.35 per 1,000 stitches depending on your brand. An 8,000-stitch logo is about $2.40 in thread — not nothing, and it adds up across a run of 50.
  2. Stabilizer and consumables. Cutaway, tearaway, water-soluble topper, needles, bobbins. Budget $0.40–$0.75 per item. Nobody remembers the stabilizer until they've done 200 pieces without charging for it.
  3. Machine time. Your machine has a finite life measured in stitches. Every job should pay a little toward its eventual replacement. If you run at roughly 600 stitches per minute, an 8,000-stitch design is ~13 minutes of runtime — price it.
  4. Your labor beyond the run. Hooping, trimming, quality checks, packing, answering the customer's emails. The machine running isn't the job; the job is everything around it. Most beginners price the runtime and donate the rest.
  5. Digitizing. Either you paid a digitizer or you spent your own evening doing it. Both are costs. Charge it on the first order, spread across the quantity.
  6. Overhead. Rent, electricity, software, the Etsy fees, the fraction of that ruined blank. Add 10% on top of your subtotal as a standing line. It won't cover everything, but it stops the slow bleed.

The test: if you can't point to each of these six in your quote and say what number you used, you're guessing. Guessing is how shops end up busy, exhausted, and somehow still broke.

Three ways shops actually price (pick one)

1. Cost-plus (recommended for most shops)

Add up the six costs above, add your margin, gross up for fees. It's the most work per quote and the most accurate. This is the method the rest of this guide uses, and it's what the free calculator automates.

2. Per-1,000-stitches rate

Set a flat rate per thousand stitches, then add the blank, digitizing, and extras. Fast to quote, easy to explain to customers. The catch: your rate has to actually cover your labor and machine time, or you're just guessing with extra steps. Back into it from the cost-plus method once — total one real job's true cost, divide by its stitch count — then use your own rate with confidence. (The per-stitch rate card we use in our shop lives in the paid system; yours should come from your numbers, not ours.)

3. Flat per-item pricing

One set price per item type, plus the garment. Works great when you do the same few jobs over and over — team uniforms, company polos. The danger is using flat rates for jobs that aren't flat: a 25,000-stitch jacket back is not a left chest logo. Use flat rates for your bread and butter, cost-plus for everything else.

The extras that should never be free

These are profit, not favors. Every one of them should be a line item on the quote:

  • Digitizing. Charged once, on the first order, as its own line item. Never absorbed, never "included." What you paid — or what your own digitizing time is worth — is the floor.
  • Order minimums. A 6-piece minimum (or a small-order fee under it) is how you stop spending 45 minutes on a $12 job. The fee should make small orders worth your setup time.
  • Rush turnaround. A tight deadline displaces other work. Price the displacement, not just the stitches — set a surcharge that reflects what rescheduling actually costs you, and apply it every time.
  • Personalization. Individual names, numbers, or monograms mean re-hooping and re-threading every piece. That is extra labor on every single item — charge it per item.
  • Specialty threads and placements. Metallic thread breaks more and runs slower. Sleeves, caps, and bags hoop harder than flat chest placements. If it costs you more time, it costs the customer more money.

Four traps that keep shops undercharging

1. Pricing off someone else's Etsy listing

The cheapest listing you can find is usually someone who forgot the six costs above. Matching their price just imports their mistake into your shop. Price from your costs, then compete on quality, turnaround, and service — the things a race to the bottom can't touch.

2. Free digitizing "to win the job"

You didn't win a job; you bought one, at the cost of an evening. Charge digitizing every time. Customers who balk at paying for digitizing were never going to be profitable customers.

3. Forgetting the platform takes its cut last

Etsy doesn't take 10% of your profit — it takes ~10% of the price. On a $26 shirt that's $2.60 out of your margin, not out of thin air. Gross every platform price up with the formula at the top of this guide.

4. Charging for materials but not for yourself

"I just do it in the evenings" still has a labor rate. If you wouldn't hoop 50 shirts for someone else for free, don't do it for your business for free. Pick a real hourly rate for yourself and put it in every quote.

The undercharging spiral: low prices → more orders → more hours → burnout → raising prices feels impossible because your whole customer base was bought with the low price. Set sustainable prices while you're small. It's ten times harder to fix later.

How to quote with confidence

The math is half the battle. The other half is saying the number out loud without apologizing for it. A few things that help:

  • Quote fast. A quote that takes three days to arrive reads as uncertain. A quote in an hour reads as professional. (This is the real reason to use a calculator instead of a spreadsheet you dread opening.)
  • Itemize. Embroidery, digitizing (one-time), and garment as separate lines gets fewer arguments than one total. People argue with totals; they accept line items.
  • Put it in writing. A simple printed or PDF quote with the design, quantity, price, and turnaround beats a text message thread every time. It also ends the "but I thought it was..." conversations.
  • Don't negotiate the method, negotiate the scope. "I can't do that price, but I can get closer if we drop the individual names" keeps your rate intact and gives the customer a choice that isn't "cheaper."

Straight answers

How much should I charge for embroidery per 1,000 stitches?
There is no universal rate that works for every shop. Back yours out of the cost-plus method once — add up a real job's true cost, divide by its stitch count, gross up for margin and fees — and you will know what a thousand stitches has to sell for in your shop. The rate card we use ourselves is in the paid Embroidery Pricing System.
How much does it cost to digitize a logo for embroidery?
Whatever digitizing cost you — a digitizer's invoice, or your own evening at your labor rate. Charge it as a separate line item on the first order and spread it across the quantity on bulk runs.
Should I charge a digitizing fee on reorders?
No. Digitizing is a one-time cost — charge it on the first order, then drop it from reorders. The reorder discount is what keeps customers coming back instead of shopping around.
What is a good profit margin for an embroidery business?
Aim for 30% or better on every job after all costs: materials, labor, overhead, and platform fees. Under 20% is the warning light — usually a labor rate that's too low or costs you're absorbing, like digitizing or rush turnaround.
How much extra should I charge for rush embroidery orders?
Enough to make pushing other jobs aside worth it — and always as a visible line item on the quote. Set the surcharge from what a rush job actually costs you, then apply it consistently instead of deciding case by case. Our own surcharge schedule is part of the paid system.
How do I price embroidery for friends and family without losing money?
Never go below true cost. A workable rule: friends and family pay your costs plus a little; everyone else pays full price. And don't give away the digitizing — free work trains people to expect free work.
Do I need to charge sales tax on embroidery?
Depends on your state — some tax custom apparel labor, others tax only the goods. Check your state's department of revenue or ask a local accountant. Collecting and remitting is always safer than guessing.
