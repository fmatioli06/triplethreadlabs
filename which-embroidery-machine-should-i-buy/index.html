Which Embroidery Machine Should I Buy? The Path I Actually Took

This question lands in the embroidery groups every single week, and most of the answers skip the only part that matters: what it is actually like to own the machine after the first month. I cannot tell you what to buy. I can show you exactly what I did: I started on a Brother PE-900, stepped up to a Brother NQ1700E, and today I run a BAi Mirror multi-needle in my own shop. This page is that path, the rules it taught me, and the honest version of every other machine people ask about, with the real prices I could verify in October 2026.

The short answer

My path, in three machines
Brother PE-900 (5x7, about $1,180): where I learned the craft
Brother NQ1700E (6x10, single needle): the workhorse years
BAi Mirror (15 needles, about $5,600 to $6,400): the shop today
Three rules came out of that path. Hoop size before brand, because the stitch field is a hard limit no accessory fixes. Budget for the year, not the machine, because the sticker is roughly two thirds of the real first-year cost. And buy for the work you will run in month six, not the work you imagine on day one.

My three machines, honestly

The Brother PE-900, where I started. Very much a beginner machine, and a great one to start on. It is a single-needle, embroidery-only machine with a 5x7 stitch field, Wi-Fi design transfer, and a price around $1,180 new. It forgave a lot while I learned hooping, stabilizer, and tension, and most of what I know about clean stitch-outs I learned on it. The ceiling showed up exactly where you would guess: one color at a time gets old on a six-color logo, and 5x7 is a hard wall. I outgrew the hoop before I outgrew the machine.

The Brother NQ1700E, the workhorse years. Same idea, grown up: still a single needle, but a 6x10 class field and a machine that ran way smoother and was much less problematic day to day. The bigger field quietly changed what I could take on, and the reliability changed how much I trusted a deadline. What it did not change: I was still the thread changer. On multi-color work I was standing at the machine swapping spools, and production speed was my two hands.

The BAi Mirror, where the shop is now. Fifteen needles, a 19.7 x 13.8 inch field, around $5,600 picked up or $6,400 delivered when I checked prices in October 2026. It has been an amazing multi-needle machine for my shop. Colors are loaded once, hats run on a real cap driver, and the machine works while I do everything else a shop needs. The honest trade, and it applies to every budget multi-needle below: support runs through the seller's online engineers and the owner community, not a local dealer. I was fine with that. Go in knowing it is the deal.

The pattern in my path is the guide in miniature: I never bought the biggest machine I could afford. I bought the next size of the problem I actually had. Field size first, then reliability, then needles, in that order, each time the work demanded it.

The hoop size rule (read this before any brand)

Every machine has a maximum embroidery area, its stitch field, and that number is set by the machine itself. A bigger hoop does not raise it. On a 5x7 machine, a 6 inch design does not fit in one hooping, period, no matter what hoop you buy for it. Your workarounds are shrinking the design, splitting it across two hoopings and lining it back up, or a multi-position hoop that shifts the fabric partway through, which works but is not the same as a native field.

Here is how the field sizes map to real work:

  • 4x4: patches, small left chest logos, baby items. The one class I see regretted within months, because most adult shirt work does not fit. (Brother PE-545, Baby Lock Aurora.)
  • 5x7: left chest, sleeves, most hats-adjacent flat work, a lot of real business. My PE-900 lived here and earned money here. It is a fine place to start and a frustrating place to stay.
  • 6x10 class: full shirt fronts, larger names and numbers, most jacket work short of a full back. This is where a single needle starts feeling like a business machine. (Brother NQ1700E, bernette b70, Baby Lock Flare.)
  • 8x14 and up: jacket backs in one hooping, big backs, quilts and blankets. On single needle that is Janome 550E territory. On multi-needle it is simply the standard field.

Watch for the phrase "multi-position hoop" in listings. A 5x12 multi-position hoop on a 5x7 machine lets you stitch a longer design in two passes by moving the fabric. Useful, but it is not a 5x12 machine, and sellers do not always say that part out loud.

Budget multi-needle vs commercial multi-needle

Here is the trade the group threads argue about, stated plainly. The budget multi-needles (Smartstitch, Poolin, BAi) put a real commercial head in your shop for $3,700 to $6,400. What you give up is the support structure: roughly a one-year warranty, help that starts online, parts shipped to you, and a learning curve that is yours to climb. My Mirror has been amazing, and I still tell people that sentence whole, because both halves are true.

The commercial names (SWF, Happy, ZSK, Barudan, Melco, and Ricoma sitting between the two worlds) charge for the company around the machine: warranties of 6, 7, and 10 years on the names above, named US service organizations, training in the deal, cap systems in the box. If embroidery is your whole income and a dead machine is a dead week, that structure is a real product and it is most of what the extra money is.

Neither side is the scam the other side's owners describe. It is a support decision dressed up as a machine decision.

What I would do at each budget

Not what you should do, what I would do, having spent my own money on three of these.

  • Under $700: I would buy the PE-545 only if my work was patches and small logos, and I would know I was renting time in the 4x4 class. Otherwise I would save toward 5x7, or shop used 5x7 machines locally with the checklist above.
  • $700 to $2,000: This is where I started, on the PE-900, and I would do it again for a first machine. If I already knew shirt fronts were my work, I would stretch to the b70 DECO's 6x10 field instead.
  • $2,000 to $4,500: Two honest roads. A top single needle (NQ1700E, Flare, or the big-field Janome 550E) if colors are simple and reliability is the priority, or the Smartstitch S-1001 if thread changes are already your bottleneck and you accept the online-support deal.
  • $4,500 to $8,000: My current neighborhood. I chose the BAi Mirror for the field and the price, knowing support starts online. If a support line matters more than field per dollar, this is Ricoma EM-1010 money, with the smaller field as the trade.
  • $8,000 and up: I would be shopping structure, not stitches: SWF for the warranty and dealer network, then Happy, ZSK, Barudan, and Melco quotes in hand before deciding. At this level every serious seller will talk. Make them.

Straight answers

Is the Brother PE-900 a good first machine for a small embroidery business?
It was mine. A 5x7 single needle at about $1,180, Wi-Fi transfer, forgiving while you learn hooping, stabilizer, and tension. Its ceiling is real: one color at a time, and nothing past 5x7 in one hooping. I outgrew the hoop before I outgrew the machine, which is the right order to outgrow things.
Is a 4x4 machine enough?
For patches and small left chest work, yes. For most adult shirt fronts and anything on a jacket back, no, and a bigger hoop does not fix it, because the field is set by the machine. It is the one size class I see regretted within months.
Can a 5x7 machine stitch a 6 inch design?
Not in one hooping. Shrink it, split it across hoopings and re-align, or use a multi-position hoop that shifts the fabric mid-design. If the work you want runs 6 inches tall, buy a field that is at least 6 inches tall.
When is it time to go multi-needle?
Watch the thread changes, not your feelings. When most jobs run four or more colors and you are standing at the machine swapping spools all day, the multi-needle pays in hours. Hats are the other trigger: a real rotating cap driver only exists on multi-needle machines. On a single needle, hats mean a flat insert and small fields.
Are budget multi-needle machines like BAi, Poolin, and Smartstitch any good?
My BAi Mirror has been an amazing machine in my shop, so yes, the machines are real. The trade is the company around them: about a one-year warranty, help that starts online, parts shipped to you, and maintenance that is yours to learn. Walk in with that picture and they are a lot of machine for the money.
How much does it really cost beyond the machine?
From the component prices I checked in October 2026: a single-needle starter typically spends another $550 to $2,000 in year one, a multi-needle setup $1,100 to $3,900. The cap setup and the software tier swing it most. The calculator above runs your version of the number.
Is Ricoma worth more than BAi or Poolin?
The extra money buys the support structure: a US company, lifetime tech support, warranty tiers to five years. It does not buy field: the EM-1010's 12.2 x 8.3 is smaller than the Mirror and EOX at 19.7 x 13.8. I picked field and price and accepted online support. Someone whose whole income rides on the machine might reasonably pick the other way.
What should I check before buying a used machine?
Stitch count from a screen photo, service records, a live test stitch with satin, fill, and outline, and a priced list of what is missing. Assume the warranty does not transfer until the manufacturer tells you otherwise, in writing. Local pickup, tested in person, is the version of used I trust.

Prices and specs on this page were checked against manufacturer and dealer listings in October 2026 and will drift. Machine packages change often. Confirm the field size, the warranty, and exactly what is in the box, in writing, before you pay anyone.
