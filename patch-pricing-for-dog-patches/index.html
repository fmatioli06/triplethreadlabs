How to Price Dog Patches (Without Losing Money on the Small Ones)

Dog patches look like easy money. They're small, customers love them, and everybody wants one with their dog's name on it. Then you quote eight bucks, spend an hour fixing the digitizing, and realize the velcro backing cost more than the patch. Here's how working shops price them so the small ones pay like the big ones.

Why dog patches are their own thing

Most patches are sized for jackets and bags. Dog patches live on vests, harnesses, and collars, usually 2 to 4 inches wide. Small does not mean cheap. A 3-inch patch can carry more stitches per square inch than a 5-inch jacket patch, because the design is denser: names, phone numbers, "DO NOT PET" text, service dog markings. On a dog patch, detail is the whole product.

Backing matters more here than on any other patch. A jacket patch gets sewn on once and stays put. A dog patch gets ripped off and stuck back on, washed, rained on, and occasionally chewed. Velcro is the default, and velcro is a real cost you have to price in.

And quantities are lumpy. One pet owner wants a single patch. A breeder wants twelve for a litter. A K9 unit or a service dog organization wants fifty of the same design. Your pricing has to handle all three without you rebuilding the quote from scratch each time.

The 5 costs that move on dog patches

Same pricing method as any embroidery work: true cost plus margin, grossed up for fees. What changes on dog patches is which costs do the moving:

  1. Stitch count from size and coverage. Measure the patch, estimate how much of it is actually stitched (coverage), and convert that to stitches. Denser designs at small sizes run high. If you're guessing the stitch count, you're guessing the price.
  2. Digitizing for small detail. Readable text at 2 inches wide takes a digitizer who knows small lettering, or your own evening. Either way it's a cost, and it belongs on the first order as its own line.
  3. Backing. Velcro costs more than iron-on, and a full velcro set costs more than hook-only. Most dog patches need velcro, so this line is bigger here than on jacket patches. Name it on every quote.
  4. The border. A merrowed border is standard on patches, and on a 3-inch patch the border is a much bigger share of total stitches than on a 6-inch one. Small patches are border-heavy; price them like it.
  5. Setup and handling. Hooping small items, trimming close, checking tiny text for errors. A run of one doesn't spread setup across anything, so singles carry full setup. That's not a penalty, it's math.

The test: if your quote doesn't name the backing, you've left the biggest variable out. "Patch, 3 inch, velcro hook" is a product. "Patch, 3 inch" is a guess.

Backing: the choice that changes your quote

Four options, four different costs. Pick with the customer, then quote exactly what you picked:

  • Sew-on. Cheapest material. The customer does the work, so it's fair for handlers who sew their own gear.
  • Iron-on (heat seal). Middle of the road. Fine for a vest that doesn't get washed hard or a patch that stays put.
  • Velcro hook. The dog-patch standard. Costs more than iron-on and worth it: the patch comes off for washing and swaps between gear.
  • Velcro set (hook plus loop). You supply both sides. Highest material cost. Quote it as its own line so the customer sees where the money goes instead of just seeing a bigger number.

The mistake is quoting one backing and delivering another, or quoting "a patch" and eating the velcro cost when the customer assumes it's included. Put the backing words on the quote and this whole problem disappears.

Small detail costs real money

A name and phone number, readable, on a 3-inch patch is dense stitching. Fine text puckers fabric, breaks needles, and punishes bad digitizing. This is the step where beginners lose money: they treat the art like it's free because the customer "just sent a picture."

Charge digitizing as a line item on the first order. On reorders of the same design, drop it. That discount costs you nothing extra and it's the reason customers come back for the second dog's patch.

If the customer's art is too detailed for the size they want, say so before you digitize, not after. Redrawing a design to survive at 2 inches wide is your time, and it's billable.

One dog vs. a whole litter

Single patches carry full setup and full digitizing. Price them like it and don't apologize. The customer buying one patch is buying custom work.

Litters, clubs, and K9 units are a different job: same design, real quantity. The digitizing gets done once, the setup spreads across the run, and the per-patch price drops while your margin holds. That's what quantity tiers are for.

Set your quantity breaks in advance and print them on the quote. Tiers end haggling before it starts, because the customer can see the next break instead of asking you for a deal.

Rush jobs (dog patches get a lot of them)

Plan for rush orders. Dog patches get rushed more than most patches: events, trials, a K9 graduation, a vest needed before a trip. When half your orders have a deadline attached, rush pricing isn't a special case, it's part of the price list.

Rush is a line item, always. It pays for the overtime and the other jobs you moved to make room. If you absorb it, you're donating your weekend. Decide your rush charge once, apply it every time, and put it on the quote where the customer can see it.

Run your own numbers

Fill this in for the job in front of you. Then run it through the free patch calculator to check your math:

  1. Size and coverage. Width × height, and your honest estimate of stitched coverage. Convert to a stitch count.
  2. Backing, named. Sew-on, iron-on, velcro hook, or full set, with its real material cost.
  3. Digitizing. First order: full line item. Reorder of the same design: zero.
  4. Labor and setup. Hooping, trimming, checking. Singles carry all of it.
  5. Overhead. Your standing percentage on top of the subtotal.
  6. Rush. If it's rush, add the line. If it isn't, don't.

True cost is the sum. Sell price is true cost grossed up for your margin target and any selling fees. That's the whole method, and it's the same math the calculator runs.

Traps that eat your margin

  1. Pricing by size alone. Two 3-inch patches can have wildly different stitch counts. Size is one input, not the price.
  2. Free digitizing on detailed designs. The denser the design, the more the digitizing is worth. Giving it away on a name-and-number patch is giving away the most skilled part of the job.
  3. Quoting one backing, delivering another. Name the backing on the quote or you'll eat the difference.
  4. Absorbing rush because the dog is cute. The dog is always cute. Charge the rush.
  5. Flat pricing across quantities. One patch and fifty patches are not the same job. Tiers protect your margin on the big orders and keep the small ones worth taking.

Straight answers

How much should I charge for a dog patch?
There is no universal number. It depends on size, stitch coverage, backing, quantity, and whether it's a first order (with digitizing) or a reorder. Run those five through the method above and you'll land on your number. If you'd rather not do it by hand, the free patch calculator runs the same math.
Velcro or iron-on for dog vest patches?
Velcro hook for anything that gets washed or swapped between gear, which is most working-dog patches. Iron-on is fine for display vests that stay put. Price the difference and let the customer choose with real numbers.
How do I price patches for a whole litter?
Same design in quantity is a bulk job: digitize once, spread the setup, and let quantity tiers bring the per-patch price down while your margin holds. Put the tiers on the quote so the breeder sees the next break.
What size are dog patches usually?
Most run 2 to 4 inches wide for vests, harnesses, and collars. Name patches are often around 3 to 3.5 inches wide so the text stays readable. Smaller than that and the lettering starts costing you in digitizing time.
Should I charge a rush fee on patch orders?
Yes, as a visible line item, set once and applied consistently. Dog patches get rushed often enough that it belongs on your price list, not decided case by case.
