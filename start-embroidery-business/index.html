How to Start an Embroidery Business (2026 Startup Guide)

Starting an embroidery business is mostly unglamorous decisions made in the right order. Machine, paperwork, pricing, customers. This guide walks the whole sequence the way working shops actually did it, and ends with a printable checklist so you can track your own progress.

The honest starting point

You don't need the big machine to start. Most working shops began with a single-needle home machine, a folding table, and jobs for people they already knew. The machine is the smallest decision. The bigger ones are pricing, paperwork, and finding repeat customers, and none of those require new equipment.

Validate demand before you upgrade. If friends of friends keep asking you to stitch things, that's a market signal. If nobody's asking, a $10,000 multi-needle won't create customers. Start lean, earn the upgrade.

Paperwork first (it's shorter than you think)

Do this before you take your first paying job. It takes an afternoon and it protects everything after:

  • Pick a name and check it. Search your state's business registry so you're not trading under someone else's name.
  • Choose a structure. Most one-person shops start as a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC. An LLC costs a filing fee and gives you separation between business and personal. This isn't legal advice, just the common pattern.
  • Get an EIN. Free from the IRS website in about ten minutes. You'll need it for wholesale accounts and the bank.
  • Open a separate bank account. Business money and grocery money live in different places from day one. Future-you will be grateful at tax time.
  • Check sales tax. Some states tax custom apparel, some tax only goods, some have exemptions for small sellers. Your state's department of revenue site has the answer, and a local accountant can confirm it in one visit.
  • A way to take payment. A simple invoicing app or payment link is enough. Don't overthink this on day one.

The rule: if you're unsure about the legal or tax side, ask an accountant, not a Facebook group. One paid hour early beats a penalty letter later.

Equipment: what you need vs. what's nice

What you need: a machine you can already run, hoops in the sizes you'll actually sell, an assortment of stabilizer (cutaway and tearaway cover most jobs), a basic thread set, spare needles, sharp snips, and cheap practice blanks. That's a working shop.

What's nice: a multi-needle machine (faster color changes, bigger hoops), a cap driver, a heat press for patches and DTF transfers, digitizing software. Every one of these earns its place once you have the order volume to feed it. Buy them with job revenue, not with hope.

Supplies are a real cost category, not an afterthought. Thread, stabilizer, needles, and bobbins go on every quote from the first one. Shops that "start charging for supplies later" usually never do.

Learn the craft before you charge for it

Run twenty practice pieces before you take money. Cheap t-shirts, tote bags, anything you can ruin without flinching. You're learning hooping, stabilizer choice, and how your machine behaves, and every mistake is cheaper on a $3 blank than on a customer's $40 hoodie.

Learn the failure modes early: puckering (usually stabilizer or hooping), thread breaks (needle, tension, or speed), and designs that were never digitized to survive at the size you're stitching. When you can look at a design and predict how it'll sew, you're ready to charge.

Pricing from day one

Price like a business from the first job, even if the first jobs are for family. The method is simple: add up your true cost (materials, labor, digitizing share, overhead), add a margin that makes the job worth doing, and gross up for any selling fees. That's it. The pricing guide walks the whole method with a fill-in worksheet.

Two rules that matter most for beginners:

  • Never price below true cost. Not for family, not for "exposure," not for your first review. Below cost isn't a discount, it's a donation.
  • Charge digitizing on the first order. It's real work or a real bill from a digitizer. Spread it across the quantity on bulk runs, drop it on reorders.

Your exact rates come from your own costs, not from copying someone else's price list. A shop in Raleigh and a shop in rural Ohio have different overhead, so their numbers differ. That's correct.

Finding your first customers

Your first three customers are almost always people you know. That's fine. Charge them properly (true cost plus a little, never below cost) and treat the job like any other: quote in writing, take a deposit, deliver on time. How you handle the easy jobs is the reputation you carry into the real ones.

After that, fish where the buyers are:

  • Local first. Teams, clubs, small businesses, churches, and schools all need embroidered gear and they'd rather buy from a person than a website. One youth league reorder beats ten one-off Etsy sales.
  • One clear offer. "Custom embroidered polos for local businesses" beats "I do embroidery." People buy specific things.
  • Show the work. Photos of finished pieces, flat and worn. Before-and-after of a tricky design. Customers buy what they can see.

Say no to jobs you can't do well yet. A declined job costs you nothing. A botched job costs you the customer and everyone they tell.

A workflow that keeps you sane

Shops don't drown from stitching. They drown from chaos: lost orders, forgotten deposits, quotes in text messages. From day one, run every job the same way:

  1. Quote in writing. Price, quantity, deadline, deposit. If it isn't written down, it wasn't agreed.
  2. Deposit before thread. Half up front is standard. It filters out the non-serious and covers your materials.
  3. Track the job. One list: quoted, in progress, done, delivered, paid. A notebook works. A tracker tool works better.
  4. Deliver and follow up. Confirm they love it, ask for a photo or review, remind them reorders skip the digitizing fee.

Traps that sink new shops

  1. Buying the big machine first. Equipment doesn't create customers. Earn the upgrade with job revenue.
  2. Pricing to "get the job." The jobs you win by being cheapest are the jobs that pay least. Price from your costs and let the wrong customers walk.
  3. Free digitizing to be nice. It's skilled work or a real bill. Charge it on the first order, every time.
  4. No deposit. Half up front, always. The customers who won't pay a deposit are the ones who wouldn't have paid at all.
  5. Tracking orders in your head. It works until it doesn't, and "doesn't" always happens during your busiest week.
  6. Skipping the paperwork. An afternoon of setup beats a tax surprise. Separate the money from day one.

Straight answers

How much does it cost to start an embroidery business?
If you already own a machine, a few hundred dollars in supplies, blanks, and filing fees gets you taking paid jobs. If you're buying the machine too, budget for the machine plus at least $300 to $500 in starter supplies and practice blanks. Start lean and upgrade from job revenue.
Do I need an LLC to start embroidering for money?
Not strictly. Many one-person shops start as sole proprietors. An LLC is the common next step because it separates business and personal liability for a modest filing fee. When in doubt, a local accountant can advise in a single visit.
What should I charge when I'm just starting out?
Your true cost plus a margin, from the first job. "Beginner pricing" that sits below cost isn't a strategy, it's a donation. The pricing guide walks the exact method with a worksheet.
Single-needle or multi-needle machine to start?
Start with what you can already run well. A single-needle machine you know beats a multi-needle you're fighting. Upgrade when order volume makes color changes your bottleneck, and pay for it with job revenue.
How do I get my first embroidery customers?
People you know first, charged properly, then local: teams, clubs, small businesses, schools, and churches. One clear offer ("custom embroidered polos for local businesses") and photos of finished work will do more than any ad at this stage.
